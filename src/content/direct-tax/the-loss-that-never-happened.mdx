## How a mutual-fund bonus can manufacture a tax loss—and what it reveals about the architecture of taxation

<BonusStrippingHero />

*The loss that never happened. A mutual-fund bonus issue can halve the NAV while doubling the number of units, leaving the investor economically no poorer. The tax problem begins when the investor sells the original units, claims the apparent loss, and keeps the bonus units.*

---

<CardPremium title="TL;DR: The Bonus-Stripping Loophole">
- **The Setup**: An investor buys mutual fund units just before a bonus issue (e.g., a 1:1 split).
- **The Event**: The bonus doubles their units, but halves the NAV. Total wealth remains unchanged.
- **The Illusion**: The investor immediately sells their *original* units at the newly halved NAV, booking a massive "loss" on paper.
- **The Reality**: They keep the bonus units (maintaining their investment value) and use the artificial loss to offset real capital gains elsewhere.
- **The Fix**: Section 94(8) now forces the loss to be ignored and added to the cost basis of the bonus units, perfectly aligning tax law with economic reality.
</CardPremium>

## A ₹10 Lakh Investment, a ₹5 Lakh Loss—and No ₹5 Lakh Has Disappeared

Imagine an investor puts ₹10 lakh into a mutual fund. He receives 10,000 units at ₹100.

The fund announces a 1:1 bonus.

The investor wakes up one morning owning 20,000 units. The NAV is now ₹50.

His statement appears to say something astonishing: he owns twice as many units, but each is worth half as much. His total wealth remains exactly ₹10 lakh.

Then he sells the original 10,000 units. He receives ₹5 lakh in cash. 

His books now appear to contain a ₹5 lakh capital loss. Yet he still owns another ₹5 lakh of the exact same economic exposure in his portfolio. 

Where did the ₹5 lakh loss actually happen?

Nowhere. It did not happen to his wealth. It only happened on paper. The wealth did not disappear; the tax system simply registered a loss. 

To understand this paradox is to understand the profound vulnerability at the heart of modern taxation: the chasm between transactional loss, economic loss, and tax loss. That three-way distinction is the intellectual spine of how financial systems are engineered, and how governments fight back.

### The Vanishing ₹5 Lakh

Let us slow down and observe the transaction mechanically.

| Moment | Units | NAV | Value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Before bonus** | 10,000 | ₹100 | ₹10 lakh |
| **After 1:1 bonus** | 20,000 | ₹50 | ₹10 lakh |
| **Sell original units** | 10,000 | ₹50 | ₹5 lakh |
| **Bonus units retained** | 10,000 | ₹50 | ₹5 lakh |

Confront the contradiction: The investor has ₹5 lakh in cash and ₹5 lakh still invested. Nothing has destroyed ₹5 lakh of wealth. Yet a transaction statement can show a ₹5 lakh loss. 

This happens because the investor is manipulating the lenses through which reality is measured.

## The Bonus Is Not Free Money

To see how the illusion works, we must first strip away the financial terminology and look at the absolute first principles of a mutual fund.

A mutual fund is nothing more than pooled assets divided by outstanding units. 
When the number of units changes without a corresponding injection of economic value, the value represented by each individual unit must mathematically change. 

If you cut a pizza into eight slices instead of four, you do not possess twice as much pizza. You possess twice as many slices, each half the size. 

The equation dictates the reality:
`Total fund value ÷ total units = NAV`

<EconomicPizzaDiagram />

If a fund holds ₹10 crore in assets and has 10 lakh units outstanding, the NAV is ₹100.
If the fund doubles the units without adding any new assets:
`₹10 crore ÷ 20 lakh units = ₹50 NAV.`

Therefore, the "bonus" created units, not wealth. This is the first conceptual unlock. 

## The Taxman Cannot Tax Your Wealth Every Second

If the investor is economically neutral, why does a loss appear? Because the tax system has a blind spot. 

Ideally, a government could theoretically tax wealth in real-time, stating: "Your net wealth increased by ₹X this year, pay us." 

But that is an administrative impossibility. It would require the continuous, instantaneous valuation of listed securities, private companies, property, businesses, jewellery, art, derivatives, and entirely illiquid assets. 

Because the State cannot observe the continuous flow of your wealth, tax systems rely on observable realization events: *purchase → holding → sale → gain/loss*. That sequence is administratively manageable. 

But this structural necessity introduces the fatal vulnerability. If tax depends on transactions, people can engineer transactions. The system is no longer measuring your economic reality; it is measuring your transactional behaviour.

## When a Tax Loss Became an Asset

A loss on paper might seem like a mere accounting quirk until you introduce the power of set-off.

Suppose Investor A has a ₹5 lakh taxable capital gain. Investor B has a ₹5 lakh capital loss. If the law allows Investor B to offset that loss against other gains, the loss is suddenly worth cold, hard money. A ₹5 lakh loss isn't merely an accounting entry—it reduces future tax liability. 

Therefore, tax losses have market value. 

Once an investor realizes this, their behavior shifts. They are no longer optimizing merely for investment returns; they are optimizing for after-tax wealth. The loss becomes an asset to be harvested.

## How to Manufacture a Loss Without Destroying the Investment

With the first principles established, the mechanical blueprint of the bonus-stripping sequence reveals itself as a brilliant, engineered arbitrage.

**The five-step mechanism:**
1. Buy units shortly before the bonus record date.
2. Receive the additional bonus units.
3. Watch the NAV artificially fall by the exact mathematical proportion of the bonus.
4. Sell the original units at an apparent loss.
5. Keep the bonus units, maintaining your economic exposure.

Economic reality remains entirely intact: ₹5 lakh cash + ₹5 lakh investment = ₹10 lakh. But the tax system has been induced to print a highly valuable capital loss. 

## The Loophole Becomes Too Elegant

The government did not wake up one morning and decide bonus stripping was morally wrong. There was an evolutionary sequence, a decades-long arms race. 

First came dividend stripping. A mutual fund would distribute a heavy dividend, which reduced the NAV. An investor would buy just before the record date, receive the tax-free dividend value separately, and then immediately sell the units at an artificially depressed NAV, harvesting a capital loss. 

The system responded. Parliament introduced Section 94(7) to disallow losses generated through dividend stripping. 

Then financial engineering simply moved. If dividends were blocked, why not use bonus units? The exact same underlying economic logic was reconstructed through a different corporate action. Bonus stripping appeared.

Parliament responded again. The 2004 Budget speech is remarkably direct. The Finance Minister explicitly stated that the government proposed to “put an end to bonus-stripping and dividend-stripping in units” through an amendment to section 94. 

The narrative is cyclical and inevitable: Taxpayer invents structure → Government closes structure → market invents another structure → Government closes that structure.

## Parliament Didn't Ban the Bonus. It Moved the Loss.

Faced with bonus stripping, the government had a choice. Why didn't they simply outlaw bonus units entirely? 

Because bonus units themselves are a legitimate corporate action. Outlawing them would break genuine financial mechanics just to stop the arbitrageurs. 

The legislative solution was much more elegant: ignore the immediate loss, and transfer that loss into the cost basis of the bonus units. 

**What the taxpayer wants:**
- Original units: ₹10L cost → ₹5L sale → **₹5L immediate loss**
- Bonus units: ₹0 cost

**What the law does:**
- Original units: ₹10L cost → ₹5L sale → **₹0 current tax loss**
- Bonus units: ₹0 original cost + ₹5L transferred basis → **₹5L tax basis**

<TaxVsRealitySplit />

The law is not saying, “Nothing happened.” It is saying, “The economic loss has not disappeared; therefore, you cannot recognize it prematurely.” The government forces the basis to teleport from the sold units to the retained units. The tax benefit is deferred until the investor genuinely exits their economic position.

## Why Three Months In—and Nine Months Out?

But how does the government differentiate between a brilliant tax-arbitrageur and a hapless retail investor who just happened to buy a mutual fund that announced a bonus? 

The government cannot read the taxpayer's mind. It cannot judge intent. So it uses behavioural proxies. 

- **Buy within three months before the record date:** Suspicious proximity. 
- **Sell within nine months after the record date:** Suspicious proximity. 
- **Retain bonus units:** Economic continuity survives.

That specific combination of actions identifies the pattern. The old section 94(8) used precisely this architecture, and AMFI's tax guidance describes the exact same three-month/nine-month framework and basis transfer mechanism.

## The Rule Cannot Read Minds

A genuine investor can potentially stumble into a bright-line condition and be penalized. Simultaneously, a highly sophisticated avoidance transaction can potentially be structured just outside the nine-month window to escape the rule entirely.

A bright-line rule is not a perfect measure of economic substance. It is a necessary compromise. 

This introduces the fundamental regulatory tradeoff: Precision vs. Administrability. 

A perfectly individualized rule would ask: “Was this particular investor genuinely trying to exploit the tax system?” But proving intent is expensive, uncertain, and litigation-heavy. So Parliament chooses an observable proxy instead. It trades perfect justice for practical enforcement.

## SAAR Is Not GAAR

The bonus stripping rule is a Specific Anti-Avoidance Rule (SAAR). 

- **SAAR (Specific Anti-Avoidance Rule):** “We have identified this particular pattern. Here is the specific consequence.”
- **GAAR (General Anti-Avoidance Rule):** “We cannot anticipate every future structure, so we reserve a broader power to attack impermissible avoidance arrangements.”

The old GAAR framework focuses on arrangements whose main purpose is a tax benefit combined with features such as misuse/abuse, lack of commercial substance, or non-bona-fide methods.

## The Rule Has Survived the Death of the 1961 Act

The Income-tax Act, 2025 came into operation on 1 April 2026, overhauling decades of tax law. Yet, the old Section 94(8) has simply been rehoused. 

It is now part of the new anti-avoidance architecture under **Section 175(9)–(10)**, titled: *“Avoidance of tax by certain transactions in securities.”* GAAR itself has been retained and housed in Chapter XI.

The rule survived the legislative rewrite because the underlying economic problem survived. The structure of reality has not changed, therefore the structure of the law must persist. 

## India Is Not Fighting an Indian Problem

Do other tax systems also struggle when investors try to realize losses without surrendering economic exposure? Yes. Different tax systems independently discovered the exact same structural vulnerability: taxpayers can manipulate the timing and identity of realization while retaining substantially the same economic exposure.

<InternationalTaxComparison />

In the **United States**, the "Wash-Sale" rule creates a firewall. If you sell at a loss and buy substantially identical securities around the sale, the loss is deferred and added to the replacement securities' basis. 
In the **United Kingdom**, "Bed-and-Breakfasting" rules use share-identification/matching to determine which shares were actually disposed of to prevent overnight loss harvesting.

The mechanism differs. The problem is recognizably the same: do not let an investor convert continuous economic exposure into an artificial immediate tax loss.

## What Exactly Is a “Loss”?

What does “loss” actually mean? 

- **Market loss:** Your asset is worth less.
- **Accounting loss:** Your recorded cost exceeds your sale proceeds.
- **Economic loss:** Your actual wealth has diminished.
- **Tax loss:** The statute allows you to use the loss to reduce taxable income.

<LossDefinitionCircles />

These four things can radically diverge. Bonus stripping is fascinating precisely because the accounting loss can be entirely real while the corresponding economic loss is completely absent. 

The tax system has to choose which version of reality it will recognize. And in the case of bonus stripping, it forcibly overrides the accounting reality to enforce the economic reality.

## Every Tax Rule Creates a Surface That Can Be Gamed

Taxation is not nature. Nature does not contain “capital gains.” Human beings created that category. 

The State creates income, deductions, losses, holding periods, exemptions, tax bases, and set-offs. These definitions are merely measurement rules. And every measurement rule creates artificial boundaries in a continuous economic universe.

Once a boundary creates a financial consequence, rational actors search for the boundary. They will walk right up to the very edge of the definition. 

## The Endless Game Between the Rulebook and the Engineer

<TaxLoopDiagram />

Tax legislation is not a finished code. It is an evolving response to human optimization. The bonus stripping rule is merely one snapshot of a ceaseless loop. 

## The Price of Perfect Economic Substance

Why doesn't the tax department simply throw out all the rules and just tax the "economic reality"? 

Because “economic reality” is highly contestable. Who determines fair value? Who decides what constitutes commercial substance, genuine investment purpose, or economic equivalence? 

The more discretionary the system becomes, the more uncertainty increases. Litigation skyrockets. Administrative discretion bloats. Compliance costs surge. 

Bright-line rules—like the three-month/nine-month window—are crude. But crude rules are sometimes administratively superior to perfect rules. That is a profound regulatory tradeoff.

## Why Rational People Keep Finding These Structures

If a tax saving equals ₹X, and the cost of restructuring your portfolio is less than ₹X, then restructuring has an overwhelming economic incentive. 

This does not require an immoral person. It requires a rational actor responding to a price signal. The phenomenon of tax arbitrage repeatedly emerges because the math dictates that it should.

## The Government Didn't Destroy the Transaction. It Reassigned Time

Section 175(9)-(10)'s architecture effectively says: You may have the transaction. You may have the cash. You may retain the bonus units. But you cannot have the tax loss now while retaining the economic value for later. 

Instead, the basis follows the surviving economic value. 

> "The law does not necessarily deny the loss. It denies the taxpayer control over when the loss enters the tax system."

The provision is fundamentally about *tax timing*, rather than simply tax denial. It is a masterpiece of legislative time-travel, teleporting a present-day loss into a future-dated cost basis.

## The Transaction Statement Can Tell the Truth—and Still Mislead You

An investor looking at a broker statement might think: *“I genuinely sold at a loss. Look at the numbers.”*

The statement is not necessarily wrong. It is merely reporting the transactional layer. The tax law operates at another layer entirely. 

This gives us a three-layer model:
- **Layer 1 (Broker statement):** What happened to the units.
- **Layer 2 (Economic portfolio):** What happened to your wealth.
- **Layer 3 (Tax computation):** What the law permits you to recognize.

The fatal mistake is assuming Layer 1 = Layer 2 = Layer 3. They don't necessarily match, and confusing the map for the territory will lead directly to a disallowed tax return.



## The Mutual Fund Was Never the Real Story

Let us return to the ₹10 lakh example. 

The investor didn't discover a magical way to destroy ₹5 lakh. He discovered something much more interesting: the tax system and the economic system were looking at the exact same portfolio through two different lenses. 

The investor tried to exploit the gap between those lenses. The State closed the gap. But the gap itself is inevitable, because economic reality is a continuous spectrum, and the law must divide it into discrete, hard-edged categories. That is why financial engineering will always exist. 

## The Loss That Never Happened

Where did the ₹5 lakh go? 

<FinalWealthCircle />

Nowhere. The investor still had it—partly as cash in hand, and partly embedded in the retained bonus units. What disappeared was not ₹5 lakh of wealth. What the investor tried to manufacture was ₹5 lakh of tax value. 

And that is why bonus stripping matters. Not because mutual funds sometimes issue bonus units. But because it exposes one of the oldest problems in modern taxation: the difference between what happened to your wealth, and what happened on paper.

## Key Takeaways

<CardPremium title="Summary of Core Principles">
1. **Financial Engineering Exploits Definitions:** A "loss" in accounting is not always a loss in reality. Tax arbitrage lives in that gap.
2. **SAAR is Surgical:** Section 94(8) is a Specific Anti-Avoidance Rule. It doesn't ban the transaction; it simply recalculates the tax math so the paper loss is ignored and added back to the cost of the bonus units.
3. **The Cycle Never Ends:** The closure of dividend stripping led directly to bonus stripping. The law is always playing catch-up to capital. 
4. **Economic Substance Matters:** Across the world (US Wash-Sale, UK Bed-and-Breakfasting, India Section 94), tax authorities are increasingly ignoring the *form* of a transaction if the *substance* shows no real economic shift.
</CardPremium>

### Actionable Checklist: Before You Call a Capital Loss “Real”

For any suspected bonus-stripping situation, do not determine tax treatment merely from the gain/loss displayed by your transaction statement. Ask these seven questions:

- [ ] 1. When were the original units acquired?
- [ ] 2. What was the record date?
- [ ] 3. When were the bonus units allotted?
- [ ] 4. When were the original units sold or transferred?
- [ ] 5. Were any bonus units still held at the time of sale?
- [ ] 6. What is the resulting economic portfolio value?
- [ ] 7. Does the statutory anti-avoidance rule apply to this timeline?
