## You Sold ₹10. The Tax System Saw ₹100.

Arjun Mehta has finally found a buyer.

Seven years earlier, he had put **₹20 lakh** into Aster Technologies Pvt. Ltd., a young software company he helped build.

For a while, Aster looked promising.

Then things changed.

Its Series C round failed.

A major customer left.

Cash reserves began disappearing.

Employees started resigning.

The company's preferred investors were unwilling to fund another round unless the founders accepted severe dilution.

Arjun wanted out.

After months of searching, an investor offered **₹10 lakh** for his 100,000 ordinary shares.

Arjun accepted.

The economics seem painfully simple.

He invested:

**₹20 lakh.**

He recovered:

**₹10 lakh.**

He lost:

**₹10 lakh.**

Then his tax adviser asks for Aster's balance sheet.

A few days later, another number appears:

**₹100 per share.**

Not the buyer's number.

Not Arjun's number.

The tax system's number.

Under the prescribed valuation methodology, assume Aster's shares have a Fair Market Value of:

**₹100 × 100,000 = ₹1 crore.**

And that changes everything.

<Ill1EconomicVsTax />

Arjun asks the obvious question:

> **How can I pay capital-gains tax when I actually lost money?**

It sounds like a question about an obscure tax provision.

It isn't.

Hidden inside it are much older questions.

**What is something worth?**

**Who gets to decide?**

**Can two people determine the tax consequences of their transaction simply by choosing a price?**

**And what should a government do when the asset being sold has no observable market price?**

To understand why ₹10 can become ₹100, we have to travel backwards.

Before Section 50CA.

Before valuation rules.

Before startups.

Almost to the beginning of taxation itself.

## The Tax Department Has a Problem: It Wasn't in the Room

Imagine another shareholder.

Call her Maya.

Maya owns unquoted shares genuinely worth roughly ₹1 crore.

Her cost is ₹80 lakh.

She transfers them to a related person for:

**₹1 lakh.**

If tax law blindly accepts the declared consideration:

Sale consideration = ₹1 lakh

Cost = ₹80 lakh

Capital loss = ₹79 lakh

The company has not necessarily lost ₹99 lakh of value.

The parties have simply chosen a low transfer price.

The resulting tax loss may then interact with the wider capital-gains system.

This reveals the state's problem.

Maya knows:

- who the buyer is;
- why the shares were transferred;
- what private negotiations occurred;
- whether there are side arrangements;
- what the company is really worth;
- whether ₹1 lakh represents genuine bargaining.

The tax department sees:

> **SALE CONSIDERATION: ₹1,00,000**

This is an **information asymmetry**.

And tax systems are extraordinarily vulnerable to information asymmetry.

<Ill2PriceMachine />

The underlying incentive is elementary:

Lower declared consideration = Lower capital gain

If taxpayers can control the first variable, they can influence the second.

This produces our first principle:

> **A sustainable tax system cannot always allow the person being taxed to choose the measurement of the tax base.**

Section 50CA was ultimately built to address one manifestation of that problem.

But why target unquoted shares?

Because quoted shares possess something extraordinarily valuable that startup shares usually do not.

A visible market.

## The Beautiful Simplicity of a Listed Share

Imagine selling 100 shares of a large listed company.

At 11:17 a.m., the exchange might show:

**Bid: ₹1,498.90**

**Ask: ₹1,499.10**

Thousands of investors may be participating.

Every transaction generates information.

One trade affects another.

Buyers compete with buyers.

Sellers compete with sellers.

The resulting market price is not metaphysical truth.

But it is **observable price discovery**.

Now ask:

> What is one share of Aster Technologies worth?

There is no Aster stock ticker.

No order book.

No continuously quoted bid.

No exchange where 5,000 independent buyers are waiting.

There may not have been a secondary sale for eighteen months.

Aster has:

- three founders;
- two venture funds;
- 41 employees with options;
- one strategic investor;
- ordinary shares;
- preference shares;
- transfer restrictions;
- liquidation preferences;
- rights of first refusal;
- board rights;
- anti-dilution provisions.

Suddenly the apparently simple question—

> **"What is one share worth?"**

—becomes surprisingly difficult.

## Aster Has Five Share Prices. None Is Necessarily a Lie.

Aster's financing history looks like this.

<Ill3FivePrices />

Which one is Aster's "real share price"?

There may be no such number.

The Series B investor paid ₹500.

But what did the investor actually buy?

Not merely a share certificate.

Perhaps the investor bought:

**one preferred share** + **liquidation preference** + **anti-dilution protection** + **information rights** + **board rights** + **conversion rights** + **negotiated exit protections.**

Arjun's ordinary share may carry a different bundle.

Therefore:

₹500 preferred share != ₹500 ordinary share necessarily.

A startup financing headline such as:

> **"Aster raises at ₹1,000 crore valuation"**

compresses an enormous amount of contractual complexity into one seductive number.

The number is useful.

It is not universal.

## The Share Is Not the Company

Suppose Aster is sold.

Its investors have a simplified **₹60 crore liquidation preference** standing ahead of ordinary equity.

Now watch what happens at different exit values.

<Ill4Waterfall />

Change the exit to ₹40 crore and the residual can disappear.

Change it to ₹300 crore and ordinary equity becomes enormously valuable.

Real preference structures can be much more complicated—participation, conversion, seniority between rounds and other rights can materially alter the waterfall.

But the underlying principle survives:

> **The value of a startup share depends not merely on what the company is worth, but on where that security sits in the economic waterfall.**

This is why "the last funding round was ₹500/share" can be a dangerously incomplete valuation argument.

## There Is No Single True Startup Valuation

We can now distinguish four ideas that everyday conversation routinely collapses into the word *valuation*.

### Transaction price
What an actual buyer agreed to pay an actual seller.

### Economic value
What an investor or analyst estimates the security is economically worth.

### Hypothetical market value
What a hypothetical market participant might pay under specified assumptions.

### Statutory FMV
What the applicable tax law tells us to use.

Now place them around Aster.

<Ill5ThreeValueTriangle />

Nothing requires those three points to coincide.

That is the intellectual foundation of the entire Section 50CA problem.

The law is not necessarily trying to discover the metaphysically "correct" value of Aster.

It needs a number that can perform a particular legal function:

**computing taxable capital gains.**

That is a different problem.

## Then Parliament Changed the Question

Before 2017, the government faced a vulnerability involving transfers of unquoted shares at artificially depressed consideration.

The **Finance Act, 2017** introduced Section 50CA into the Income-tax Act, 1961.

Its architecture was conceptually straightforward.

Where the consideration received or accruing from transfer of a share of a company other than a quoted share was below the prescribed FMV, that prescribed FMV could be deemed to be the full value of consideration for computing capital gains.

The CBDT's contemporary explanation placed the measure within the effort to address tax avoidance through undervaluation of unquoted shares. 

From **1 April 2026**, India's rewritten income-tax legislation carries this architecture into **Section 79 of the Income-tax Act, 2025**.

So Section 50CA is now the historical label by which many practitioners recognise the problem.

For current transactions, Section 79 is the statutory home.

The philosophy survived the rewrite.

## What the Law Changes—and What It Doesn't

Suppose Arjun and the buyer agree:

**₹10/share.**

The share purchase agreement says:

**₹10/share.**

The buyer transfers:

**₹10/share.**

Arjun receives:

**₹10/share.**

The company's register records the transfer.

Section 79 does not need to rewrite their commercial agreement to create its tax consequence.

Instead, it can operate inside the capital-gains computation.

<Ill6FourRealities />

This is subtle but enormously important.

> **Freedom to choose a commercial price is not necessarily freedom to choose the tax base.**

Modern legal systems routinely allow different bodies of law to attach different measurements to the same economic event.

## Inside the Black Box: How the State Manufactures a Number

Under the current framework, Rule 57 of the Income-tax Rules, 2026 provides the prescribed valuation machinery for relevant unquoted equity shares.

At its core sits an adjusted net-asset-value architecture broadly represented by:

FMV = (A + B + C + D - L) × (PV / PE)

The variables make adjustments for specified asset categories, securities, immovable property, liabilities and paid-up equity.

The important conceptual point is not memorising the letters.

It is understanding what the machine is doing.

It begins with information that the tax administration can verify.

Then it transforms that information according to prescribed rules.

Finally it allocates the resulting equity value to the relevant share.

Let's put Aster through the machine.

<Ill7TaxNumber />

That ₹100 is not necessarily:
- Aster's latest funding price;
- Arjun's negotiated price;
- an investment bank's DCF;
- the price another founder obtained six months earlier.

It is the result produced by the **statutory measurement system**.

That distinction matters enormously.

## The Formula Knows the Balance Sheet. Aster Lives in the Future.

Now imagine a different Aster.

Its economic engine is software.

Its balance sheet shows:

**Visible financial position**
Cash: ₹5 crore  
Equipment: ₹1 crore  
Other relevant assets: ₹1 crore  
Liabilities: ₹3 crore

Simplified net assets:
**₹4 crore**

But Aster also has:
**10 million users**, **₹50 crore recurring revenue**, **60% growth**, **proprietary software**, **valuable customer data**, **strong network effects**, **three strategic acquirers watching it.**

A venture investor might value those future economics at:
**₹300 crore.**

<Ill8TwoWays />

This exposes the deepest tension in startup valuation:

> **The balance sheet is primarily an organised record of what exists and has happened. A startup investor is often paying for what might happen next.**

That does not make balance-sheet valuation useless.

It explains why statutory value and venture-market value can diverge.

## Why Not Just Use DCF?

At this point the obvious objection appears.

Why doesn't the government simply value every startup according to its future cash flows?

Because the future does not come with audited accounts.

Consider two competent valuers.

Which one should the tax officer accept?

Now imagine hundreds of thousands of private-company transactions.

The problem becomes apparent.

A highly sophisticated valuation method can improve economic sensitivity while simultaneously increasing:
- discretion;
- compliance cost;
- expert disagreement;
- administrative burden;
- litigation.

A mechanical formula sacrifices some nuance but buys something valuable:
**reproducibility.**

## The State Had Three Bad Choices

The policy problem can now be seen clearly.

### MODEL A — Trust the actual price
**Strength:** closest to the cash transaction.
**Weakness:** potentially manipulable.

### MODEL B — Reconstruct full economic value
**Strength:** economically richer.
**Weakness:** subjective, expensive and disputable.

### MODEL C — Prescribe a valuation mechanism
**Strength:** consistency and administrability.
**Weakness:** the model can diverge from lived economics.

India's architecture substantially chooses **Model C** for the problem addressed here.

The choice becomes much easier to understand once we stop assuming that the legislature's only objective is perfect valuation accuracy.

Governments also optimise for auditability, enforceability, consistency, compliance cost, and resistance to manipulation.

## The Distress Sale: When the Market Moves Faster Than the Formula

Now return to Aster—but let the company deteriorate.

<Ill10NinetyDays />

Now suppose the prescribed FMV relevant to the transaction nevertheless works out to:
**₹100.**

What is the more informative number?

The statutory formula says: **₹100.**
The only buyer in the actual market says: **₹40.**

This is not necessarily evidence that the taxpayer cheated.

The ₹40 transaction itself may contain new information:
> **Aster's probability of failure has increased dramatically.**

The difficult policy question is therefore not: "Is FMV good or bad?"

It is:
> **How much weight should a tax system give to an actual distressed-market transaction when its prescribed valuation proxy produces a substantially different number?**

That is much harder.

## A Market Transaction Is Also Information

Economists often think of price as an output.

But price is also a signal.

A buyer willing to pay only ₹40 may be telling us something.

Perhaps:
- the company's funding prospects collapsed;
- its technology became obsolete;
- customer concentration became dangerous;
- litigation appeared;
- a licence was threatened;
- future dilution became almost certain;
- the shares are deeply illiquid.

The statutory valuation model may eventually incorporate some of those developments.

But economic information does not always enter accounting systems instantaneously.

That creates a timing problem.

The market can sometimes reprice an asset faster than the accounting data used by a valuation formula.

This is one reason unlisted equity is inherently difficult to value.

The OECD's current international statistical framework explicitly acknowledges that difficulty and uses several methods to approximate the market value of unlisted equity, including recent transaction prices and own-funds/book-value approaches depending on the circumstances. 

The world has not discovered a magic formula.

It has developed competing approximations.

## Britain Puts a Hypothetical Buyer in the Room

The United Kingdom illustrates a different philosophy.

For relevant unquoted-share valuation questions, HMRC's framework asks what the asset might reasonably fetch in a hypothetical open market and relies on specialist Shares and Assets Valuation expertise.

This is more economically textured.

But the sophistication comes at a cost.

Who is the hypothetical buyer? What information do they possess? How large is the shareholding? Does it confer control? What discount applies to a minority interest? What restrictions attach to it?

HMRC itself treats unquoted-share valuation as specialist work. 

The uncertainty hasn't disappeared.

It has moved from the **formula** into **professional judgement**.

## Australia Asks a Different Question

Australia provides another instructive comparison.

Its market-value substitution framework in relevant capital-gains situations places substantial importance on whether parties dealt at **arm's length**.

That introduces a question India's more mechanical comparison can make less central:
> **Was this a genuine bargain between economically independent parties?**

Imagine two Aster transactions.

**Transaction A**
Founder sells to his brother. No negotiation. Price: **₹10.**

**Transaction B**
Founder runs a three-month sale process. Seven investors review the company. Two bid. Founder accepts the best bid. Price: **₹40.**

Both can be below some independently calculated FMV.

But Transaction B contains much stronger market evidence.

Australia's approach demonstrates the attractiveness of considering the quality of bargaining—but also why doing so makes administration more fact-intensive. 

Again: **accuracy rises.** So can **complexity.**

## Singapore Changes the Question Entirely

Singapore provides the most radical comparative insight.

Genuine capital gains are generally outside its income-tax base, subject to important distinctions where gains are revenue/trading in nature and to specific statutory regimes. 

That means Singapore does not confront every ordinary share disposal through precisely the same capital-gains architecture as India.

This reveals a principle bigger than valuation:

> **Every valuation rule is downstream of a decision about what society wants to tax.**

If the tax base changes, the valuation problem changes.

The valuation system is therefore not independent infrastructure.

It is the child of tax policy.

## The Phantom Gain Is More Precise Than It First Appears

Return to our original numbers.

Arjun:
**Cost:** ₹20 lakh  
**Cash received:** ₹10 lakh  
**FMV:** ₹1 crore

The "phantom difference" is:
₹1 crore - ₹10 lakh = ₹90 lakh

But the taxable capital gain is not automatically ₹90 lakh.

Why? Because capital gains involve the cost basis and other applicable computation rules.

In our simplified illustration:
₹1 crore - ₹20 lakh = ₹80 lakh

So we should distinguish:
**Phantom consideration uplift (₹90 lakh)** from **Illustrative capital gain (₹80 lakh)**

That distinction becomes obvious in another example.

<Ill11Substitution />

The rule has reduced the capital loss.

It has not created a capital gain.

This is why the technically precise description is:

> **FMV substitutes for the relevant consideration figure.**

The phantom gain appears only when that substituted consideration exceeds the applicable cost base sufficiently to produce a positive gain.

## Now Make the Example Brutal

Suppose Arjun's facts are worse.

**Cost:** ₹50 lakh
**Actual distressed sale:** ₹5 lakh
**Prescribed FMV:** ₹2 crore

Economic result: ₹5L - ₹50L = -₹45L
Tax computation before other applicable adjustments: ₹2Cr - ₹50L = ₹1.5Cr

<Ill12LiquidityParadox />

This is the provision at its most psychologically jarring.

The taxpayer can lack the cash corresponding to the taxable number.

The economic problem is no longer merely **tax rate**.

It becomes **tax liquidity**.

The liability can arise from a value the taxpayer never monetised.

That is what gives the phantom-gain problem its bite.

## Then the Buyer Walks Into the Same Trap From the Other Side

Suppose Aster's prescribed FMV is **₹100/share** and Arjun transfers 100,000 shares for **₹10/share.**

Actual transfer value: **₹10 lakh**
FMV: **₹1 crore**

The seller-side valuation rule is only one half of India's anti-undervaluation architecture.

The current Income-tax Act also contains an inadequate-consideration regime potentially relevant to the recipient of specified property, including shares, subject to the statutory conditions and exceptions.

<Ill13TwoSidedVice />

The architecture has a clear logic.

If only the seller were addressed, value could potentially be shifted cheaply to the buyer.

If only the buyer were addressed, the seller might still engineer a low capital-gains consideration.

So the law can examine both sides of the same value transfer.

## The ₹1 Bad-Leaver Clause

Now Aster's shareholder agreement creates an even stranger situation.

Suppose Arjun becomes a contractual "bad leaver." The SHA requires him to transfer **10,000 shares** at **₹1/share.**

He receives: 10,000 × ₹1 = ₹10,000

Suppose statutory FMV is **₹150/share.**
Then the statutory value is: 10,000 × ₹150 = ₹15,00,000

<Ill14OneShareTwoSystems />

The shareholder agreement may be commercially valid.

The contractual penalty may have been deliberately negotiated.

But contract law does not automatically dictate tax valuation.

That yields an important transaction-design principle:

> **A contractual transfer formula should never be drafted without modelling the tax consequences of the transfer it may eventually compel.**

A ₹1 bad-leaver clause can be economically punitive.

A valuation rule can make it fiscally punitive as well.

## "But We Already Have a Valuation"

Few sentences should worry a sophisticated adviser more.

Which valuation?

Suppose Aster has these documents:
- Last preferred funding round: ₹500
- ESOP exercise analysis: ₹120
- Financial-reporting fair value: ₹180
- Secondary investor offer: ₹70
- Tax-rule FMV: ₹240

Someone says:
> **"The valuation is ₹500."**

That sentence contains almost no useful information until we ask:

**For what purpose?** **For which security?** **On what date?** **Under which law?** **Using which methodology?** **With which rights?** **For which transaction?**

The mature way to think about private-company valuation is therefore not Company -> One Value, but rather contextually based on the security, purpose, date, and methodology.

Value is contextual.

## Valuation Is Becoming Regulatory Infrastructure

Aster's founder once thought valuation happened during fundraising.

That worldview is obsolete.

Valuation can matter during share issuance, secondary transfers, founder exits, employee transactions, cross-border transfers, restructurings, buybacks, acquisitions, shareholder disputes, bad-leaver events, and financial reporting.

And different legal regimes may ask different valuation questions.

A transaction can simultaneously touch **income tax**, **company law**, **FEMA**, **stamp duty**, **accounting**, **shareholder agreements**, and **investor rights.**

The modern cap table is therefore not merely a spreadsheet showing percentages.

It is an architecture of economic rights sitting inside several overlapping measurement systems.

## The State Is Not Taxing Reality. It Is Taxing a Model of Reality.

Now we can finally see the full chain.

At each stage, information is compressed.

The founder experiences **uncertainty.**
The investor sees **probability.**
The accountant sees **financial statements.**
The valuer sees **methodology.**
The lawyer sees **rights.**
The tax administrator sees **a taxable base.**

No institution sees the entire economic reality.

Each constructs a representation suited to its purpose.

The statutory FMV is one such representation.

## Why Civilization Needed a Number

Strip away the statutory language.
Strip away Aster.
Strip away venture capital.

What remains?

Human beings possess scarce things. Scarce things acquire value. Ownership creates rights over those things. Humans exchange those rights. Exchange creates economic gains and losses.

States need resources. States tax some economic gains. Taxation requires measurement. Measurement requires information.

Information is unevenly distributed.

The taxpayer often knows more about the private transaction than the government.

People respond to incentives. If private parties can freely choose the number determining the tax base, some will rationally choose numbers that reduce tax.

The state therefore constructs proxies. But every proxy introduces approximation error.

And approximation error creates two possible injustices.

## The Two Errors the State Can Never Completely Eliminate

### Error One — Under-taxation
A ₹100 asset is deliberately transferred for ₹10.
The government accepts ₹10.
The tax base is artificially suppressed.

### Error Two — Over-taxation
A genuinely distressed ₹40 transaction occurs.
The statutory model produces ₹100.
The taxpayer is taxed using a value never actually realised.

These errors pull in opposite directions.

Reduce Error One aggressively and you risk increasing Error Two.
Reduce Error Two aggressively and you may reopen opportunities for Error One.

That is the real policy frontier.

Section 79 represents a choice about where India wishes to sit on that frontier.

## The Question Is Not Whether the Formula Is "Correct"

That framing is too primitive.

The real questions are:

**Correct for what purpose?**
**Compared with what alternative?**
**At what administrative cost?**
**With what opportunity for manipulation?**
**With what risk of genuine hardship?**

A DCF may better model Aster's future. But it creates assumption risk.
A recent transaction may better capture current distress. But it can be manipulated between related parties.
Book-derived inputs may be auditable. But they can miss intangible economics.
A hypothetical-market test may be conceptually elegant. But it demands expert judgement.

There is no valuation architecture without trade-offs.

That is why different countries choose differently.

## The Founder Should Reverse the Transaction Workflow

The unsophisticated workflow is:
Negotiate Price -> Sign SPA -> Transfer Shares -> Receive Money -> Call CA -> Discover FMV Problem.

By then, economics have already been locked in.

The sophisticated workflow reverses the logic:
What Security? -> What Rights? -> Which Tax Rule? -> What Valuation Date? -> What Statutory FMV? -> What Buyer-Side Consequences? -> What Other Regulatory Values? -> Why Does Commercial Price Differ? -> Model Tax Liquidity -> Negotiate -> Document -> Close.

That is not tax compliance after the transaction.

It is **tax-aware transaction design**.

## The Nine Questions Before a Single Share Moves

### **1. What exactly am I selling?**
Ordinary equity, preference shares and other securities can carry very different economics.

### **2. What rights travel with that security?**
Voting, liquidation preference, conversion, transfer restrictions, anti-dilution and exit rights can affect economic value.

### **3. Which statutory valuation regime applies?**
Do not assume that a valuation prepared for another purpose answers the income-tax question.

### **4. What is the relevant valuation date?**
Valuation is time-specific. A startup can deteriorate dramatically in months.

### **5. What is the prescribed FMV before the agreement is signed?**
This is the number most likely to reveal the phantom-consideration risk.

### **6. Why does the commercial price differ from statutory FMV?**
A large difference deserves a serious explanation.

### **7. What happens to the buyer?**
A seller-side analysis is incomplete if the acquisition itself creates an inadequate-consideration issue.

### **8. What other laws impose valuation or pricing requirements?**
Tax is only one layer.

### **9. Where will the cash come from if taxable consideration materially exceeds actual proceeds?**
This is the question that converts valuation risk into **liquidity risk**.

## The Real Alpha: The Dangerous Number Is the One You Discover After Signing

Most explanations of Section 50CA reduce the lesson to:
> **Do not sell shares below FMV.**

That is too shallow.

A genuine commercial transaction may have excellent reasons to occur below a statutory value.

The deeper lesson is:
> **Never discover the statutory FMV only after the commercial price has become irreversible.**

That changes everything.

Suppose the buyer offers **₹40/share.**
Before accepting, the founder discovers: **Statutory FMV = ₹100/share.**

The ₹60 gap now becomes a transaction variable.

The founder can investigate:
- Is the buyer discounting minority status?
- Is the security illiquid?
- Is there a contractual restriction?
- Has Aster deteriorated?
- Is dilution imminent?
- Is there a preference overhang?
- Is the buyer exploiting distress?
- Can the structure legitimately change?
- What is the seller's resulting tax liquidity?
- What happens to the buyer?

The valuation report has ceased to be a compliance certificate.

It has become **decision intelligence**.

That is the real upgrade in thinking.

## Aster's Final Share

At the end of our story, Arjun owns one final Aster share.

Ask six people what it is worth.

The buyer says:
> **₹10.**

Arjun says:
> **₹10 is the best price I can actually obtain.**

The venture investor says:
> **You cannot compare my preferred share with his ordinary share.**

The valuer says:
> **It depends on the methodology and valuation purpose.**

The accountant opens the balance sheet.

The tax statute produces:
> **₹100.**

None of them necessarily lied.

They answered different questions.

The error is assuming that all six questions should produce one number.

## When Money, Law and Reality Disagree

Section 50CA—and now Section 79—looks at first like a narrow anti-avoidance provision concerning unquoted shares.

Seen from far enough away, it becomes something much larger.

It is a case study in the limits of measurement.

A private economy contains millions of facts that the state cannot directly observe.

Negotiations. Fear. Optimism. Distress. Information. Relationships. Optionality. Control. Liquidity. Probability.

The legal system cannot feed all of that directly into a tax return.

It needs categories.
It needs formulas.
It needs numbers.

So reality is compressed.

And sometimes compression creates distortion.

That is the price of making a vast tax system administrable.

The founder experiences a business failure. The balance sheet records assets and liabilities. The buyer observes risk. The valuer constructs a model. The legislature constructs another.

And eventually all of that complexity collapses into one cell in a tax computation:
> **Full value of consideration: ₹1,00,00,000.**

Yet only ₹10 lakh ever entered the founder's bank account.

That is why the phantom gain is more than an obscure startup-tax trap.

It reveals something fundamental about modern government:

> **The state cannot tax economic reality directly. It must first translate reality into a measurable legal representation.**

Sometimes that representation tracks the market beautifully.

Sometimes it prevents blatant manipulation.

And sometimes it produces a number that the human being on the other side of the transaction barely recognises.

A founder sees: **₹10.**
A buyer sees: **₹10.**
The tax system may see: **₹100.**

The deepest question is therefore not:
> **What is the share worth?**

It is:
> **Who gets to define "worth" when negotiated price, economic value and statutory reality refuse to agree?**

That was the problem Section 50CA attempted to solve.

From 1 April 2026, **Section 79 inherits the problem.**

And no valuation formula can make the underlying dilemma disappear.

For transactions governed by the new legislation from 1 April 2026, the corresponding architecture is contained in **Section 79 of the Income-tax Act, 2025**, read with the applicable valuation rules in the Income-tax Rules, 2026. The illustrations intentionally simplify facts—including preference waterfalls, capital-gains calculations and valuation inputs—to isolate the underlying economic and legal principles. Actual transactions require analysis of the precise security, valuation date, applicable exemptions, acquisition cost, holding period, tax rates, corporate documents, residence of the parties, cross-border rules and other relevant provisions. The comparative examples are likewise conceptual rather than assertions that foreign regimes are identical to India's. The point of comparison is to reveal the different design choices available when governments confront the same underlying problem: **how to value an asset for which no continuously observable market price exists.***
