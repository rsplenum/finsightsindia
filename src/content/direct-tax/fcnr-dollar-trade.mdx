<CardPremium title="TL;DR: The FCNR Dollar Trade">
- **The Rate:** In mid-2026, Indian banks began offering unusually high rates (7–7.5%) on 3-to-5-year US-dollar FCNR(B) deposits.
- **The Reason:** India was experiencing external pressure and needed foreign currency. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) introduced a concessional swap facility that subsidised the cost for banks to hedge these dollar deposits.
- **The Economics:** Banks weren't just paying for dollars; they were paying for the RBI-subsidised opportunity to transform those dollars into domestic liquidity. 
- **The Truth:** The NRI is effectively selling India the "time-use" of their dollars. The high yield is the price of that scarcity.
</CardPremium>

There is a moment in every financial story when a number becomes so unusual that it forces you to stop looking at the number and start asking what is happening underneath it. In the summer of 2026, that number was 7.5%. A non-resident Indian with dollars could, at some Indian banks, earn around 7.5% a year on a three-to-five-year US-dollar FCNR(B) deposit. Ujjivan Small Finance Bank was advertising 7.5% in July, while Equitas Small Finance Bank was offering as much as 7.52% for certain USD deposits of $10,000 and above. ([The Economic Times](https://m.economictimes.com/markets/forex/forex-news/nris-looking-to-park-your-dollars-ujjivan-sfb-offers-7-5-dbs-up-to-5-6-on-fcnr-deposits/amp_articleshow/132136210.cms?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That sounds like an ordinary banking promotion.

It wasn't.

A dollar is already a valuable asset. It can sit in a US bank account, a US Treasury bill, a money-market fund or another dollar-denominated instrument and earn a market return without exposing its owner to the rupee. So why would an Indian bank suddenly be willing to pay such a high rate for the privilege of holding that dollar? And why would the Reserve Bank of India simultaneously make it easier—and cheaper—for Indian banks to obtain those dollars?

The answer takes us far beyond the question of whether an NRI should open an FCNR deposit. Because the 2026 FCNR episode is really a story about scarcity. India was short of something it could not manufacture.

Dollars.

And once you understand why dollars are scarce for India, why an Indian bank cannot simply manufacture them, why an NRI possesses something the Indian banking system desperately wants, and why RBI was willing to change the economics of the transaction, the extraordinary interest rate begins to make sense. More importantly, something else becomes visible. The NRI is not merely being offered a deposit.

The NRI is being offered a price for bringing a scarce resource into India's financial system. And that makes the FCNR trade much more interesting.

### A strange proposition

Imagine that you live in Dubai, London, Singapore or New York and have accumulated $1 million. You are not looking to speculate on the rupee. You do not want to convert your wealth into Indian rupees.

You simply want your money to remain dollar-denominated. Under normal circumstances, your decision is relatively straightforward. You compare the yields available on dollar assets.

You can hold a US Treasury.

You can hold a bank deposit.

You can use a money-market instrument. You can invest in other dollar assets. The world gives you a menu of places in which your dollar can live.

Now imagine an Indian bank tells you: We will pay you 7% or more on your dollars. The first reaction should not be:

"Great. Free money." It should be: "Why does this bank value my dollar so much?"

That is the question from which the entire FCNR story unfolds.

### The dollar is not just another currency

To understand the trade, we have to go backwards.

India can create rupees.

The Reserve Bank of India is the issuer of the country's monetary base. It can create rupee liquidity and influence the domestic price of money. But RBI cannot manufacture US dollars. If an Indian company imports $10 million of machinery, the foreign supplier does not accept newly created rupees as settlement simply because the buyer's central bank can create them.

The supplier wants dollars.

If an Indian oil company needs to pay an overseas seller for crude, it needs dollars or another acceptable foreign currency. If an Indian company has borrowed dollars abroad and its debt comes due, it needs dollars. If an Indian resident wants to acquire a foreign asset, dollars may be required.

This creates a fundamental asymmetry. India controls the supply of: ₹ but not the supply of: $ That is the first principle. A country can create its own currency. It cannot create somebody else's currency merely by wanting it.

And that is why foreign exchange is fundamentally different from domestic liquidity.

### The invisible product India is buying

Once you understand that distinction, an FCNR deposit looks different. An NRI who brings $1 million into an Indian bank is not merely making a deposit. Economically, the NRI is bringing into India something that the Indian financial system cannot produce on its own:

a foreign-currency resource.

The Indian bank receives the dollar. The NRI receives a dollar-denominated claim against the bank. The bank can then manage that foreign-currency liability through its treasury operations and, under the special 2026 arrangement, through the RBI's concessional swap facility.

This is why FCNR(B) exists in the first place. It allows eligible non-residents to maintain term deposits in permitted foreign currencies rather than converting their wealth into rupees. RBI's framework describes FCNR(B) as a fixed-deposit account for NRIs and PIOs, denominated in permitted freely convertible currencies. ([System Health](https://systemhealth.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_ViewMasDirections.aspx_id%3D10198%282%29.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) That feature is extraordinarily important.

Because it means the NRI can bring the dollar to India without accepting ordinary rupee currency exposure on the principal.

### The crucial difference between FCNR and NRE

This is where many explanations of FCNR become unnecessarily confusing. Suppose an NRI has: $100,000 and converts it into rupees to invest in an NRE deposit. The investment is now fundamentally a rupee asset.

If the rupee falls against the dollar, the NRI's dollar-equivalent wealth can decline even if the NRE deposit itself earns interest.

FCNR changes that equation.

The deposit remains denominated in the chosen foreign currency. If the NRI places: $100,000 in a USD FCNR deposit, the principal is still a dollar claim. The NRI therefore does not need to make the same rupee-directional bet merely to earn an Indian-bank deposit rate.

This is one reason FCNR can be attractive to a dollar-earning NRI. It offers exposure to the Indian banking system without converting the deposit itself into a rupee liability. That is a subtle but powerful distinction.

So why would India pay more?

Now we reach the interesting part. If a dollar can earn a return elsewhere, an Indian bank has to compete for it. The bank cannot simply say:

"Please bring us your dollars." The NRI can say: "Why should I?"

That question creates a market. Suppose a dollar can earn 4% elsewhere. An Indian bank offering 3% has little reason to expect a large migration of dollar deposits merely because the bank is Indian.

But if it offers 6%, the conversation changes.

At 7%, it becomes unusual.

At 7.5%, it becomes something investors start talking about. And that is precisely what happened in 2026. But the rate is only half the story

There is an important misconception here. One might imagine that Indian banks simply became unusually generous.

They didn't.

Banks are not charitable institutions. If a bank pays: 7% for dollars, the bank must believe it can deploy those dollars or transform the funding into something economically valuable enough to justify paying the depositor. Normally, that transformation has a cost.

And this is where the Reserve Bank of India enters the story.

### The hidden price of a dollar

An Indian bank receiving a dollar deposit faces a problem. Its liabilities are in foreign currency. But much of its natural business is in rupees.

The bank wants to lend to: Indian companies; households;

infrastructure projects; businesses;

consumers.

Most of those borrowers want rupees. So the bank potentially faces a transformation problem: How do I take a dollar liability and turn it into economically useful rupee funding without taking an unacceptable currency risk?

The answer is the foreign-exchange market. A bank can use swaps and other hedging instruments to transform its currency exposure.

But hedging costs money.

And that cost can become significant when global dollar funding is expensive or when the market is under stress. This is the invisible number behind the FCNR interest rate.

### Think of the bank as a currency transformer

Imagine an Indian bank receives: $100 from an NRI. The bank owes: $100 to that depositor at maturity. But the bank would prefer to use the economic value of that funding in India's rupee economy.

So the bank enters the FX market. Conceptually: USD → INR today, with a corresponding obligation to reverse the currency position later.

The bank has therefore transformed the currency. But transformation creates a price. That price is reflected in the swap market.

And this is where RBI's 2026 intervention becomes crucial.

### RBI changed the economics of the trade

In June 2026, RBI introduced a special swap facility for banks against fresh FCNR(B) deposits of three-to-five-year maturity. The central bank effectively offered banks a concessional way of hedging the foreign-currency deposits they mobilised. Contemporary reporting described the arrangement as effectively covering the banks' hedging cost through the concessional swap. ([Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/explained/explained-economics/fcnrb-deposits-rbi-swap-scheme-rates-nri-inflows-10731940/lite/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

At the same time, RBI temporarily relaxed the normal interest-rate restrictions on these deposits. That combination is the real story. RBI did not merely say:

"Banks may pay more." It effectively said: "Banks may pay more—and we will make the foreign-exchange economics of obtaining those dollars more attractive."

That is a completely different intervention.

### This is why 7% is not the whole story

Suppose a bank could normally earn an economic return of 6% from deploying the funding but had to pay 5% for the dollar and another 1% to hedge it. The economics are unattractive. Now suppose RBI reduces the effective cost of the FX transformation.

The bank can afford to bid more aggressively for the dollar. The NRI sees only one side of the transaction:

7% interest.

But behind that rate sits an entire institutional architecture. The bank's treasury sees: deposit cost + FX hedge + liquidity + asset deployment + regulatory treatment.

RBI sees something even broader: foreign-currency inflow + reserve management + balance-of-payments support + future liabilities. This is why the apparently simple FCNR deposit is actually a macroeconomic instrument.

The NRI sees a deposit. RBI sees a dollar. This difference in perspective is the key to understanding the entire episode. For the NRI:

"I am earning 7% on my dollars." For the bank: "I have acquired foreign-currency funding at a price that has become economically attractive."

For RBI: "The Indian financial system has attracted foreign currency at a time when external liquidity matters." Three people can look at exactly the same transaction and see three completely different things.

And all three interpretations can be correct.

### The extraordinary rate becomes less extraordinary

This is the point at which the 7.5% headline loses some of its magic. It is still a high dollar return. But once you understand the policy architecture, you realise that the rate is not simply a reflection of what an Indian bank naturally thinks a dollar is worth.

It is partly a reflection of policy-created economics. RBI temporarily changed the price signals surrounding foreign-currency deposits. That is why banks could offer rates that looked extraordinary relative to ordinary global dollar deposit rates.

In July, for example, Ujjivan Small Finance Bank was offering 7.5% on USD FCNR(B) deposits for three-to-five-year maturities, while Equitas was offering as much as 7.52% for qualifying deposits. ([The Economic Times](https://m.economictimes.com/markets/forex/forex-news/nris-looking-to-park-your-dollars-ujjivan-sfb-offers-7-5-dbs-up-to-5-6-on-fcnr-deposits/amp_articleshow/132136210.cms?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) The market was responding to the policy. And then something even more interesting happened

The market responded very strongly. By August 13, banks had mobilised approximately:

The policy was designed to attract dollars.

It attracted them.

A lot of them.

So much so that RBI decided it did not need to keep the special FCNR window open for the full originally announced period. The central bank had effectively reached: "We have enough."

### The market had answered RBI's question

The policy experiment can be understood almost like a controlled economic experiment.

RBI changed the incentives.

Banks responded by increasing the price they were willing to pay for foreign currency. NRIs responded by moving dollars into Indian banks. The inflow became large enough to alter RBI's policy decision.

The feedback loop looked like this:

This is central banking in a form that rarely makes it into personal-finance articles. The interest rate is only the visible surface.

Underneath it is a policy feedback system. Why were NRIs willing to move the dollars? The answer is more interesting than "because the rate was high."

The NRI is comparing risk-adjusted dollar returns. Suppose the alternatives look something like: US Treasury instruments;

US bank deposits; money-market funds; corporate bonds;

Indian FCNR deposits.

The NRI is not asking: "Which gives the highest headline percentage?" The NRI is asking:

"What return am I being paid for taking the additional risks associated with this investment?" Those risks include: Indian bank credit risk;

reinvestment risk; liquidity; regulatory conditions;

counterparty risk; opportunity cost; future interest-rate conditions.

And there is one particularly important fact: FCNR does not eliminate Indian bank credit risk. The deposit is a claim on the bank.

The dollar denomination protects the depositor from ordinary rupee-denomination risk on the principal, but it does not transform the Indian bank into a US Treasury. That distinction matters enormously.

### The NRI is therefore selling something

What exactly is the NRI selling?

Not the dollar itself.

The NRI is effectively selling the use of the dollar for a period of time. In exchange, the NRI receives: interest;

the bank's credit promise; contractual maturity; foreign-currency denomination.

The transaction can therefore be thought of as:

This is the economic heart of the trade. And that makes the maturity crucial

A dollar deposit for three months is one thing. A dollar deposit for three to five years is another. The longer maturity gives the Indian banking system something particularly valuable:

stable foreign-currency funding. RBI's special 2026 facility was designed specifically around fresh FCNR(B) deposits in the three-to-five-year maturity range. ([Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/explained/explained-economics/fcnrb-deposits-rbi-swap-scheme-rates-nri-inflows-10731940/lite/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Why?

Because India did not merely need dollars today.

It wanted stable dollars.

A three-to-five-year liability is much more useful for managing a medium-term external-financing problem than a deposit that can disappear next month. The longer maturity therefore gives RBI and the banking system breathing room. But it also creates something else.

A future obligation.

That is the subject of Article III.

### There is another reason the trade is attractive: Indian tax treatment

For a qualifying non-resident, the interest on approved foreign-currency deposits with a scheduled bank can be exempt from Indian income tax under Section 10(15)(iv)(fa) of the Income-tax Act, subject to the statutory conditions. The Income Tax Department expressly lists such foreign-currency deposit interest as exempt for non-residents and certain not-ordinarily-resident individuals/HUFs. ([Etds](https://incometaxindia.gov.in/documents/left%20menu/other%20non-resident.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) That matters because the headline rate is not necessarily the after-tax rate. If an investor compares:

### 7% FCNR

with: 5% taxable dollar investment, the comparison should not be made simply by looking at the two nominal percentages.

The relevant question is the after-tax, risk-adjusted dollar return. This is one reason FCNR can become genuinely competitive for the right NRI. But "tax-free" does not mean "risk-free"

This distinction is essential. A tax exemption increases the attractiveness of the return. It does not eliminate:

bank credit risk; reinvestment risk; liquidity constraints;

opportunity cost; global interest-rate risk;

policy changes.

And the most important risk for an NRI is often psychological rather than mathematical: What if I lock my dollars into a three-to-five-year instrument and the world changes? Because the special rate is attached to a particular maturity.

If dollar rates abroad rise sharply, the opportunity cost of remaining locked in may increase. If global rates fall, the FCNR may become extraordinarily attractive. If the Indian bank's creditworthiness changes, the risk assessment changes.

The trade therefore has a time dimension.

### The one-year lock-in is another clue

Banks' special 3–5-year FCNR offerings can also have lock-in conditions around premature withdrawal, depending on the product and applicable rules. This is not a trivial administrative detail. It tells us something about what the bank wants.

The bank is not merely trying to collect dollars.

It wants stable funding.

The economics of the policy are therefore built around the idea: Bring us the dollar—and leave it here for a meaningful period. That is precisely why the three-to-five-year maturity is so important.

### The dollar trade can therefore be viewed as a bargain

The NRI says: "I will give you my dollars for several years." The bank says:

"I will pay you an unusually attractive return." RBI says: "I will make the bank's foreign-exchange transformation cheaper."

India says: "In return, I get foreign-currency liquidity now."

Everyone gets something.

But everyone is also taking a risk. The NRI takes bank and opportunity-cost risk. The bank takes balance-sheet and funding risk.

RBI takes policy and future settlement risk. India takes the risk that the foreign-currency liability eventually becomes difficult to refinance or repay. This is why the transaction is more sophisticated than its appearance suggests.

### Now consider the NRI who borrows the dollars

This is where the FCNR story becomes particularly fascinating. Suppose an investor does not have $1 million of spare cash. Instead, the investor can borrow dollars at: 4% and place them in an FCNR deposit earning: 7% The apparent spread is: 7%-4%=3%. On $1 million, that is: $30,000 of gross annual spread before costs, taxes where applicable, fees and other risks.

Suddenly the FCNR product isn't merely a savings product. It becomes the potential foundation of a carry trade.

Borrow cheaply.

Deposit at a higher rate.

Capture the spread.

This is where the story moves from personal finance into financial engineering. But the spread is not free money Suppose the borrowing cost rises from 4% to 6%.

The spread falls from: 3% to: 1%. Suppose the FCNR rate falls at renewal. Suppose the borrowing rate is floating. Suppose the bank imposes fees.

Suppose the investment cannot be exited when desired. Suppose the counterparty becomes less attractive. The apparent arbitrage can disappear.

The trade therefore depends on the persistence of a funding-rate differential. And that is exactly why sophisticated investors care so much about the policy mechanism behind the FCNR rate. If the high rate exists because RBI temporarily subsidises the economics, then the trade may be highly attractive today but very different at maturity.

### The most important distinction: opportunity versus subsidy

A normal financial product becomes attractive because its underlying economics are attractive. The 2026 FCNR opportunity is different. Part of its attractiveness was deliberately created by policy.

That does not make it fake.

It makes it conditional.

The rate is real.

The interest is real.

The tax treatment can be real. The bank's obligation is real. But the extraordinary economics are linked to a temporary policy regime.

That means the investor must distinguish between:

### the return available today

and: the return that will be available when the money has to be reinvested. That is a completely different question.

### This is where many investors make the wrong calculation

An NRI may look at:

### 7.5% × 5 years

and conclude: "This is a spectacular dollar investment." But that ignores the entire opportunity-cost curve.

The real question is: What is the five-year expected return relative to alternative dollar assets, after tax, after credit risk, after liquidity constraints and after accounting for the fact that the policy-supported rate may not exist when the deposit matures? A high entry yield is not the same thing as a permanently high return.

### The FCNR trade is therefore a bet on time

The NRI is effectively locking in today's policy-created economics. That can be extremely valuable. If the rate remains attractive relative to global dollar yields, the investor wins.

If global dollar yields fall, the FCNR can become even more attractive. If global dollar yields rise dramatically, the opportunity cost increases. But there is another party making a different bet.

RBI is betting that the foreign currency it attracts today is more valuable to India than the cost of the future liability. This is the beginning of the macroeconomic story.

### The same dollar has two prices

This is one of the most useful ways to think about FCNR. For the NRI, the dollar has an:

investment price.

That is the return the NRI could earn elsewhere. For India, the dollar has a:

scarcity price.

That is the economic value of having access to foreign currency when the country needs it. These two prices do not have to be equal. If India is under external pressure, the scarcity value of the dollar can rise substantially.

India may therefore be willing to pay an NRI a rate that looks generous relative to ordinary dollar deposits. The high rate is telling us something. It is telling us that:

the marginal dollar has become more valuable to the Indian financial system.

### The 2026 FCNR boom was therefore a price signal

The rate itself became a message. It told global Indian capital:

Bring your dollars here.

And the response tells us something about the market. NRIs did not need to be persuaded by patriotism.

They needed to be paid.

That is the beauty of market design. RBI did not order NRIs to transfer their dollars. It changed the incentives so that the transaction became attractive enough for them to choose it.

### This is how a central bank can influence private capital without controlling it

The RBI cannot command: "NRIs, send $50 billion." It can only change the economic environment.

It can make the transaction: more profitable; cheaper to hedge;

less constrained; more attractive relative to alternatives. The private investor then makes the decision.

This is a recurring principle of financial-system design: The state often moves capital not by commanding investors, but by changing the prices they face. The FCNR programme is a particularly clean example.

### The trade also contains a subtle asymmetry

The NRI can think: "My deposit is in dollars, so I don't care about the rupee."

That is only partly true.

The principal may remain dollar-denominated. But the Indian banking system does care enormously about the rupee because the bank is using the funding inside an Indian financial system dominated by rupee assets. The bank therefore needs to manage the currency transformation.

And RBI needs to manage the resulting liquidity and foreign-exchange position.

The NRI sees a clean product.

The financial system sees a chain of conversions. That is why the FCNR deposit cannot be understood independently from the FX swap.

### The bank's invisible calculation

A bank deciding whether to aggressively bid for FCNR deposits is effectively asking: What is my all-in cost of acquiring this foreign currency, and what can I earn from using it? That calculation includes more than the advertised deposit rate.

It can include: deposit interest; FX swap economics;

regulatory treatment; liquidity; balance-sheet costs;

credit exposure; operational costs; the return on the assets against which the funding is deployed.

RBI's intervention changed some of these variables simultaneously. That is why the policy could produce such a large behavioural response.

### This is why the FCNR programme is actually a bank incentive programme

The public sees:

NRI gets 7%.

But the policy is really targeted at: the bank's willingness to bid for dollars. RBI doesn't need to negotiate with every NRI.

It only needs to change the economics faced by banks. Banks already have: NRI customer bases;

branches abroad; remittance networks; treasury operations;

distribution channels.

The banks then transmit the policy into the retail market. This is why the commercial bank is the critical middle layer.

### The machine in Article II begins here

Article II will follow the dollar after the NRI has made the decision. Because once the NRI says: "Yes, I will bring my dollars."

the story stops being primarily about personal finance. The dollar enters the banking system. The bank now has to manage it.

RBI becomes involved.

The foreign-exchange market becomes involved. The balance of payments becomes involved. And the central bank's reserves become involved.

The apparently simple deposit has become a macroeconomic instrument. But before we leave the NRI, there is one more question

Why now?

Why did India suddenly need to make FCNR so attractive in 2026?

Because the timing matters.

India had experienced significant external pressure in the first half of 2026. The rupee had weakened substantially, while foreign-exchange reserves had fallen from earlier highs before recovering as policy-driven inflows arrived. By July, commentary on the FCNR measures was explicitly linking the programme to the programme to the need to attract foreign currency and support India's external position. ([Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/opinion/the-fcnr-window-what-rbi-s-measures-mean-for-nri-depositors-13980151.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) And the external environment remained difficult. India's July 2026 merchandise trade deficit widened to nearly $32 billion, with elevated crude prices adding to the import bill, even as services remained a major source of external earnings. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/indias-july-merchandise-trade-deficit-3198-billion-2026-08-13/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That is the deeper reason FCNR suddenly became valuable. India did not wake up one morning and decide: "Let's give NRIs 7.5%."

It faced an external-financing problem. And one of the quickest things a country can do when it needs foreign currency is to ask: Who already owns dollars and might be willing to bring them here?

The answer is obvious.

Its diaspora.

### India has an extraordinary foreign-currency asset sitting outside its borders

Millions of Indians and people of Indian origin live abroad. They earn: dollars;

dirhams; pounds; euros;

Singapore dollars;

other foreign currencies.

Much of that wealth naturally remains where it was earned. India cannot force those dollars home.

But it can create incentives.

The FCNR mechanism is one such incentive. That makes the Indian diaspora something more than a remittance source. It becomes a potential foreign-currency funding pool.

And this is one reason FCNR is strategically interesting.

### Remittances and FCNR are not the same thing

This distinction is worth making carefully. A remittance is generally money sent to India for consumption, family support, investment or other purposes. An FCNR deposit is a financial claim.

The NRI is not necessarily giving up the economic ownership of the money. The NRI is placing the money into an Indian bank under a contractual deposit arrangement. This makes FCNR much closer to foreign-currency borrowing by the banking system from the diaspora than to an ordinary remittance.

That is precisely why the maturity matters.

A remittance comes in.

An FCNR deposit comes in with a claim attached to it. And that claim is the hidden cost This is where the high rate becomes intellectually interesting.

India gets: foreign currency today but creates: foreign-currency liability tomorrow. The NRI gets: interest and retains: a dollar claim. So the transaction is not: "India received free dollars." It is:

"India rented dollars for a period of time." That is perhaps the simplest way to understand the entire programme. And once you phrase it that way, the next question becomes unavoidable:

What happens when the rental period ends?

### The trade is therefore attractive precisely because India needs time

This is the hidden bargain.

The NRI receives an attractive dollar return. The bank receives stable foreign-currency funding. RBI receives greater flexibility in managing the external balance.

India receives time.

But time has a cost.

The deposit matures.

The dollar can leave.

The interest has to be paid.

The hedge has to unwind.

The banking system has to settle its foreign-currency obligations. And if many deposits mature at the same time, the demand for dollars can become macroeconomically relevant. This is why the end of the FCNR trade is ultimately more important than its beginning.

### The investor should therefore ask five questions

Before an NRI looks at the headline rate, the proper framework is:

**First:** What is my alternative dollar return?

The relevant benchmark is not an Indian rupee deposit.

It is another dollar asset.

**Second:** What risks am I accepting for the extra yield?

The premium exists for a reason.

**Third:** How much of the return survives after tax and costs?

The Indian tax exemption can be valuable for qualifying non-residents, but the comparison should still be made on an after-tax basis.

**Fourth:** How liquid is my money?

A three-to-five-year deposit is not the same thing as a Treasury bill that can be sold tomorrow.

**Fifth:** What happens at maturity?

Today's rate is known.

Tomorrow's renewal rate is not. That final question is the one most promotional comparisons ignore.

### The most seductive mistake

The seductive mistake is to see:

### 7.5%

and compare it with:

### 4%

somewhere else.

Then conclude: "India gives me 3.5% extra." But the correct question is:

What exactly am I being paid that extra 3.5% for? Once you ask that question, you begin to see the real structure. You are being compensated for:

bank credit exposure; reduced liquidity; policy dependence;

opportunity cost; and, from India's perspective, the value of obtaining your dollar funding. The premium is not a free lunch.

It is a price.

And prices reveal scarcity.

### The deepest interpretation of the 2026 FCNR rate

The 7% rate is therefore not merely a deposit rate. It is a signal from the financial system. It says:

The marginal dollar is valuable enough to India that the system is temporarily willing to pay an unusually high price to attract it. That is why this story is bigger than NRIs. The rate is telling us something about India's external balance sheet.

It is telling us that foreign currency had acquired enough strategic value for RBI to intervene in the economics of dollar funding. And the size of the response tells us something else: There was a substantial pool of foreign currency outside India that could be persuaded to move when the price became attractive enough.

But the machine has now done what it was designed to do By mid-August, the response had become so strong that RBI decided to close the special FCNR swap window early. The policy was supposed to run until September 30.

Instead, the FCNR window will close on August 31. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/indias-central-bank-close-fx-deposit-swap-facility-prematurely-2026-08-14/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) That is a fitting end to the first chapter.

The NRI was offered a price.

The NRI responded.

The dollars came.

RBI observed the response.

And RBI pulled back the incentive.

The market had cleared.

But that is where the real story begins Because the dollar has now entered India.

The NRI has made the trade.

The bank has accepted the liability. RBI has facilitated the currency transformation. India has obtained the foreign-currency liquidity it wanted.

But the transaction has created a question that no interest-rate advertisement can answer: What happens when the NRI wants the dollar back? That is not a question about the deposit rate.

It is a question about India's balance sheet. It is a question about reserves. It is a question about the rupee.

It is a question about the current account. It is a question about exports, remittances and capital flows. And ultimately it is a question about whether India used the time purchased by FCNR to strengthen the very system that generates dollars in the first place.

### The first article ends where the investor's decision ends

The NRI sees an unusual proposition: A dollar deposit in an Indian bank paying as much as 7–7.5%. The headline looks like an investment opportunity.

The deeper analysis reveals something more interesting. India is effectively saying: "Your dollar is unusually valuable to us right now. Leave it with our banking system for several years, and we will pay you for the privilege."

The NRI must then decide whether the premium compensates for the risks.

That is The Dollar Trade.

But once the NRI says yes, the story leaves the investor's hands. The dollar enters the Indian banking system.

And another machine begins.

<CardPremium title="Summary of Core Principles">
1. **Dollars Cannot Be Printed:** A country can create endless domestic liquidity, but it cannot manufacture the foreign currency required to settle external obligations.

3. **FCNR is a Bridge:** The programme converts immediate foreign-currency scarcity into a future foreign-currency liability. India is buying time.
