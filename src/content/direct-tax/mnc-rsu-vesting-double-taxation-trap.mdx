On a brisk November morning, a senior software engineer at Microsoft India opened his Morgan Stanley portal. After four years of grinding through performance reviews, his first major tranche of Restricted Stock Units (RSUs) had finally vested. He was granted 100 shares. But staring back at him on the screen was a deposit for exactly 69 shares.
</p>

His first instinct was panic. Had the broker made a mistake? Had the company reneged on the contract? 

He wasn't robbed. He was simply experiencing the brutal, dual-pronged execution of the Indian Income Tax Act. For employees at multinational tech giants like Google, Meta, and Amazon, RSUs are marketed as the golden ticket to generational wealth. But beneath the shiny offer letter lies a tax dragnet so aggressive that it literally sells your assets before you even own them.

The moment an RSU vests, the Indian government does not view it as a stock. They view it as pure, unadulterated salary. 

Under Section 17(2), the total market value of those 100 shares is instantly added to your taxable income as a "perquisite." If the shares are worth ₹10 Lakhs, your salary just artificially jumped by ₹10 Lakhs on that specific day. Because you are a high-earning tech employee, you sit squarely in the 30% tax bracket. The government demands their ₹3 Lakh cut immediately.

But since you haven't sold the shares, you don't have the cash to pay the tax. To prevent you from defaulting, foreign brokers like E*Trade or Schwab execute a ruthless maneuver known as **Sell-To-Cover**. They automatically liquidate 30% of your newly minted shares, send the cash directly to the Indian government as TDS, and hand you the leftovers.

The psychological shock of seeing a third of your wealth evaporate is just the beginning. 

If you decide to hold onto those remaining 69 shares hoping for the stock to double, you walk straight into the second prong of the tax trap. Because these shares belong to a US-based company (not listed on the NSE or BSE), the Indian tax code classifies them as **Unlisted Foreign Assets**. 

When you eventually sell them, you don't get the gentle 12.5% tax rate afforded to domestic stocks after one year. You must hold these foreign shares for a gruelling 24 months just to qualify for Long-Term Capital Gains. If you sell before that two-year mark, every dollar of profit is added right back to your salary and taxed at your 30% slab rate. You are effectively taxed as a high-frequency trader.

Even buying shares at a discount is weaponized against you. 

Many MNCs offer an Employee Stock Purchase Plan (ESPP), allowing you to buy company stock at a 15% discount. It feels like free money. But the moment those shares hit your account, the tax algorithm triggers again. That 15% discount is classified as a perquisite. The government demands you pay 30% tax on the discount itself, forcing you to pay tax on money you haven't even made yet.

> *"I didn't sell anything. I just let the shares sit there. Three years later, the Income Tax Department sent me a notice for ₹10 Lakhs. They said I was holding illegal foreign assets."*

The most devastating trap of the RSU ecosystem has nothing to do with capital gains. It has to do with a piece of legislation passed to hunt down offshore billionaires: **The Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) Act, 2015**.

When you hold RSUs in a US brokerage account, you are legally holding a foreign asset. The law mandates that you must explicitly declare these shares in a heavily scrutinized section of your Income Tax Return known as **Schedule FA** (Foreign Assets). 

The algorithm does not care if you only hold two shares of Apple worth $300. It does not care if you haven't made a single rupee of profit. If you fail to check the Schedule FA box and meticulously report the peak value of your foreign brokerage account, the Black Money Act dictates a mandatory, non-negotiable penalty of ₹10,00,000. 

A simple oversight by a hurried CA can wipe out an entire year of your savings. 

<NoticeTrap title="The Form 16 Blindspot">
Never assume your employer has handled everything. Your company will deduct the 30% TDS on vesting and report it in your Form 16. But they will **never** file Schedule FA for you. The burden of declaring the foreign asset lies entirely on your shoulders.
</NoticeTrap>

The golden handcuffs of MNC compensation are real, but they are entirely navigable if you refuse to play blind.

When vesting day arrives, anticipate the Sell-to-Cover slaughter. Do not let the missing shares induce panic; verify the exact TDS remitted on your Form 26AS. If you hold the shares, map out the 24-month horizon before you sell to secure the Long-Term Capital Gains rate. And above all, fire any CA who attempts to file your ITR-2 without demanding the complete transaction statement from your foreign broker. 

Because in the eyes of the algorithm, ignorance isn't innocence. It's just a ₹10 Lakh invoice waiting to be mailed.
