The Packaging Audit

The engines are more honest than almost anything shipping in Indian personal finance. The packaging is not yet worthy of them. This is the gap, measured — twenty findings with file-and-line evidence, then what to build, how to get found, and whether any of it can win.

Scope homepage · 4 calculators · 54 articles · design system · SEO · AdSense Method source-read, not screenshot-read Branch fix/learning-loop-integrity-and-calculator-correctness

The verdict, first

You have solved the hard half and left the easy half undone

I read the source rather than the screenshots: global.css, the four calculator pages, the eighteen rung components, all 54 articles' frontmatter, the layout, the content schema, the build output, and the doctrine and solutions files. Every number below is reproducible with a command.

What I found is a project with an unusual shape. The correctness work is genuinely exceptional — sol-046 caught a page printing 19.20% where the truth was 4.09%; sol-043 caught the tax page recommending the losing regime; dd-013 forbids two surfaces disagreeing about one number. I do not know of another Indian personal-finance site with this discipline. Most of them are wrong on purpose, because being wrong sells a product.

But the doctrine has been applied almost entirely to what the numbers say and almost not at all to what the screen looks like. The visual layer is running on conventions inherited from an earlier product — one called "SWP Intelligence Engine" — that nobody has audited with the same rigour. The result is that the most honest calculator in the market is wearing the costume of a Bloomberg terminal, and asking a 55-year-old to read 9px uppercase letterspaced labels to find out whether their retirement works.

Part I — The visual system

Eight cross-cutting defects, every one of them global

These are not page bugs. Each one is a decision encoded once in src/styles/global.css or repeated by habit across every screen, which means each is also fixable once. They are ordered by how much reading effort they cost.

F-01 Blocker

The interface whispers: 317 pieces of type below 12px

The pages are built almost entirely out of micro-labels. Every section heading on the tax calculator is 10px. Every regime tag is 9px. The insurance page's three answer-card labels are 10px. These are not captions — they are the primary navigation of the page.

$ grep -roE "text-\[(9|10|11)px\]" src/ | wc -l 317 tax-calculator.astro 34 Rung components (18 files) 52 sip-engine.astro 25 insurance-analyzer.astro 16 swp-planner.astro 14 index.astro 3

The audience is people planning a retirement. Presbyopia begins around 40 and is universal by 55. A 9px uppercase letterspaced string is not small text to them — it is an obstacle, and the thing behind it does not get read. You have a doctrine entry, dd-003, that says the user pays in time and attention. This charges them and returns nothing.

Set a floor of 13px for any label carrying meaning, and 15–16px for anything that is really a heading. The four scale classes already in global.css (.text-hero, .text-section, .text-card-title, .text-caption) are good — the problem is that pages bypass them with arbitrary bracket values 317 times. Add a fifth (.text-micro at 13px), then make an arbitrary text-[Npx] a lint failure. This is one sweep and it changes the felt quality of every screen.

F-02 Blocker

The signature gold fails WCAG AA — on every page, at every size

text-gold-600 is #B8941F. On white that is 2.88:1. The threshold for normal text is 4.5:1. It carries every eyebrow on the site: "Financial decisions, made clear", "Start here", "Income tax", "Retirement income", "Direct tax". It is the brand, and it is unreadable to anyone with moderately reduced contrast sensitivity — which, again, is the retirement audience.

Since light is the default for most first-time visitors arriving from Google, the failing theme is the one most people see.

This report's own accent is that corrected gold, so you can see it costs nothing in character.

F-03 Major

Everything shouts: 317 uppercase letterspaced runs

uppercase tracking-widest appears 317 times. On the insurance page, the panel headings are "1. THE INSURANCE POLICY'S PROMISE", "2. THE SAFE DIY ROUTE", "3. THE GROWTH DIY ROUTE", "POLICY & RISK INPUTS", "TRUE YIELD (XIRR)", "TOTAL PAID OUT", "THE SAME MONEY", "MODERATE YIELD", "RUNS OUT · YEAR 30", "FUNDS THE PROMISE" — ten all-caps strings visible in one viewport.

All-caps is measurably slower to read: it destroys word-shape, which is what fluent readers actually recognise. It is a device for one thing on a screen — the label that must be found before anything else. Used 317 times it emphasises nothing and merely raises the volume of the whole page. This is dd-007 — every visual difference must mean something — inverted.

Cap it at one all-caps element per card and one per section. Everything else becomes sentence case at a real size. The heading "1. The insurance policy's promise" set at 15px semibold sentence case reads faster, looks more expensive, and stops competing with the numbers — which are the thing you actually want found.

F-04 Major

Ten formatters, two rupee conventions, both on one page

formatRupee() is defined ten separate times across the codebase, plus formatShortRupee() and formatIndianRupeeLabel() in utils/formatters.ts. The answer layers were hand-written in prose and use a different convention from the shared utility.

Answer layer (prose): ₹1.28 crore ₹89.1 lakh ₹1.00 crore Shared formatShortRupee: ₹ 1.50 Cr ₹ 28.85 Lakh ₹ 2.91 Cr Both appear on the SWP planner, ~1200px apart, describing the same kinds of money. Differences: leading space · abbreviation vs word · capitalisation · 1dp vs 2dp.

You have dd-013: two surfaces may never show different numbers for the same input. This is its presentation-layer twin — two surfaces showing the same number in different clothes. A reader cannot tell whether "₹ 1.50 Cr" and "₹1.28 crore" come from the same system, and the doubt is corrosive precisely because the rest of the site works so hard to be trustworthy.

One module, one convention, every surface. My recommendation: the word, not the abbreviation ("₹1.28 crore"), no space after the symbol, one decimal place above a lakh. Delete the ten local copies. Add a test that fails if a formatRupee is declared outside utils/ — the same shape of guard you already used for the CAGR literal in sol-047.

F-05 Major

916 KB of PDF library loads on two pages before anyone asks for a PDF

html2pdf.js is a static top-level import on both engine pages. Chart.js is correctly lazy-loaded on the same files, so the pattern is already known and simply was not applied here.

src/pages/swp-planner.astro:650 import html2pdf from 'html2pdf.js'; ← eager src/pages/sip-engine.astro:526 import html2pdf from 'html2pdf.js'; ← eager src/pages/insurance-analyzer.astro:396 const { Chart } = await import('chart.js/auto'); ← lazy ✓ dist/_astro/html2pdf.C76KHe0e.js 916 KB

On a mid-range Android on Indian 4G that is roughly a second of download plus several hundred milliseconds of parse, on the main thread, before the Monte Carlo worker even starts. It will show up directly in Core Web Vitals, which is a ranking input, on the two pages you most want to rank.

Move it inside the click handler: const { default: html2pdf } = await import('html2pdf.js'). Ten minutes of work, and it removes about 60% of the JavaScript on both pages.

F-06 Major

Six dead elements on the SWP planner, one of them visibly stuck

Six ids are declared in the HTML and never referenced again anywhere in src/. One of them ships a visible, permanent loading message beside a gold info icon — it is in your own screenshot.

swp-planner.astro:390 <div id="tier3ActionableFooter">Calculating actionable financial guidance...</div> repo-wide references: 1 (the declaration itself — no script ever writes to it) Also dead: sacrificeTitle · sacrificeIcon · sacrificeMetric1 · sacrificeMetric2 · wowMetricBanner

A spinner that never resolves is worse than no spinner. It tells the reader the page is broken, on the one screen where you most need them to believe the numbers.

Delete all six. If the guidance line was a real intention, it belongs in the answer layer, which already does that job properly.

F-07 Minor

Whitespace is generous between sections and cramped inside them

Section padding is py-14 sm:py-20 — around 56–80px, well judged. Inside the cards it collapses: space-y-1 between a label, a ₹-figure and a sub-caption in the tax answer cards, gap-3 in the homepage's numbered trio. So the page breathes at the macro scale and holds its breath at the scale where reading actually happens. The visible effect is the "dense terminal" feel in every screenshot — lots of air around blocks of compressed data.

Raise intra-card rhythm to space-y-2/space-y-3 and let cards grow. Vertical space inside a card is cheaper than the reader's parsing effort — that is dd-009: remove work, not information.

F-08 Minor

Three font families load; the mono is declared but barely used

Layout.astro imports @fontsource/geist-sans and @fontsource/geist-mono at full weight ranges, while global.css imports four Inter weights and sets Inter as the body face — so Geist Sans downloads on every page and is never used. Meanwhile --font-mono is applied only through .stat-value and .input-premium; most ₹-figures on the answer layers are Inter with tabular-nums.

Drop the Geist Sans import. Then decide deliberately: either every rupee figure on the site is mono, or none is. Right now it is arbitrary, which makes some numbers look like data and others like prose with no rule behind the difference — dd-007 again.

Part II — Screen by screen

The Answer layer landed on four pages; the layers under it landed on two

The single most important structural fact about this product: the "Answer + rungs" pattern is genuinely finished on the SWP planner and the SIP engine, and is bolted on top of an untouched legacy dashboard on the tax calculator and the insurance analyser. Everything below follows from that.

Homepage — the strongest page on the site

src/pages/index.astro is 157 lines and does its job. "What are you trying to decide?" with five question-shaped cards is exactly right: it addresses the reader's intent rather than your product taxonomy, and "You do not need to learn the vocabulary before you begin" is a promise the rest of the site mostly keeps. The "What you leave with" aside is a good idea — showing the form of the output before the reader commits.

Three things hold it back:

  • The five card glyphs are meaningless. ₹ ⌁ ↗ ◇ ⌇ — a lightning-ish mark for retirement, a diamond for insurance, a squiggle for options. These are decoration wearing the costume of information (dd-007/dont-1). Either use five real icons that depict the decision, or drop them and let the questions carry the cards. The questions are good enough to.
  • The hero costs a full viewport and adds one sentence of information. Your own T1 item said "cut page weight so the first real choice is above the fold" and marked it done, but on a 1080p laptop the five decision cards still begin below the fold. Compress the hero by about 40%.
  • Nothing shows what an answer looks like. The aside describes the output in quotes. A far stronger move is a live one — see Idea 1.

SWP planner — the pilot, and it shows

The top of this page is the best work in the project. "You would typically finish with ₹1.28 crore left — but this plan is tight. About 1 in 3 futures run dry before 30 years are up, which is more risk than most people want to carry into retirement." That is a complete answer, in a sentence, with the uncertainty carried honestly and the frame stated ("in today's prices"). Then three cards that each name a real cost. I would not change a word.

The expert rung below it is a different product. "The Survival Score · 70.25% · LIFESTYLE SACRIFICE ACTIVE · MODERATE RISK · 1.9X LIFETIME PAYOUT · Dynamic Guardrails Active" — six competing status indicators, three of them badges, all caps. Then a table whose headers are 10px all-caps: DURATION / TOTAL GROSS / TAXES PAID / NET IN-HAND / BAL REMAINING, with a red error dot next to the tax figure. Then the dead "Calculating actionable financial guidance…" (F-06).

F-09 Major

"70.25%" is precision the model cannot support

A 10,000-path Monte Carlo has a standard error of roughly ±0.5 percentage points on a survival estimate near 70%. Re-run the same inputs with a different seed and you get 69.8%, or 70.9%. Two decimal places asserts a resolution the simulation does not have, and it invites the reader to treat 69.9% and 70.3% as different answers.

Print 70%, or better, the sentence the answer layer already uses: "about 1 in 3 futures run dry". You have dd-010 — measure the lived experience, not the accountant's summary. The answer layer obeys it; the expert rung does not.

SIP engine — the same pattern, well executed

"You typically finish with ₹89.1 lakh, not ₹1.00 crore" is a superb line: it names the shortfall as the answer rather than burying it in a projection. "About 42 in 100 futures reach the goal" and the three levers ("Put away ₹3,000 more" / "Give it 3 more years" / "Aim for ₹89.0 lakh instead"), each with its own honest failure count, is the clearest presentation of a savings trade-off I have seen on an Indian site. "Tap one to apply it" is the right affordance.

Two frictions. The three levers are visually identical, so the reader must read all three to discover that one of them is a retreat rather than an effort — consider a quiet distinction between "try harder" and "want less". And "Assuming 13.3% growth with 28.4% swings, 6% inflation, and 70% of it in shares" is four numbers in one sentence, three of which the layperson cannot evaluate; "28.4% swings" in particular is an unexplained concept, which is dd-004. Move the roughness figure behind the "Change these" link and keep growth and inflation in the sentence.

Tax calculator — the answer is right, and then the page says it all again

The regime comparison is rendered twice, ~400px apart, in two visual languages

The answer layer shows New ₹97,500 / Old ₹1,35,720 with a "Cheaper for you" tag. Scroll one screen and the legacy "Interactive Slab Engine" shows New ₹97,500 / Old ₹1,35,720 again, in different cards, with "FY 25-26" chips and "NEW REGIME SAVES ₹38,220". Both are visible in a single screenshot in your own capture.

This is dd-006 — never make the reader diff two states — committed against your own answer layer. The reader's question is not "do these agree?" but they will ask it anyway, because two boxes showing the same number is what a spreadsheet looks like when something has gone wrong. Delete the two regime cards and the "NEW REGIME SAVES" chip, collapse what remains into a "See the full computation" rung matching the SWP planner's, and let the answer layer be the only place a total appears.

F-11 Major

The input form asks 20+ questions before the reader knows which matter

The left column runs: Income & Demographics, HRA Exemption (Old Regime), Deductions (Old Regime), "Do you have any of these too?" — every field visible at once, each headed by a 10px all-caps label. But the entire old-regime block only matters if the reader's deductions can exceed the break-even the answer layer computes.

Follow the pattern your two best pages already prove: three questions (salary · rent paid · 80C so far), the answer, then "Do you have any of these too?" as a progressive rung. The answer layer already states the break-even ("you would need ₹1.54 lakh more") — use it to gate the form, not just to explain it. Most readers can stop after three fields, and telling them so is the product.

Insurance analyser — the best headline, the most confusing body

"Buy the cover on its own and invest the difference. You collect the same income this policy promises, in the same years, and finish with ₹52.52 Lakh more" is the single most valuable sentence on the website. It is the one comparison the whole subject reduces to (dd-005), it protects against the obvious objection ("both routes pay you the same income, in the same years"), and it will genuinely stop people buying bad endowment policies. That paragraph is the product.

F-12 Major

Two different quantities, both bold, both labelled "in today's prices", 500px apart

The answer says the surplus is ₹9.14 Lakh in today's prices. The panel below says "THE SAME MONEY / in today's prices: ₹9.43 Lakh" — which is the policy's total payout, an entirely different quantity. The phrase "in today's prices" is attached to at least four distinct figures on this one page.

insurance-analyzer.astro:195 "The same money / in today's prices" ← total payout ₹9.43 L InsuranceAnswer.astro:84 "in today's prices" ← surplus ₹9.14 L insurance-analyzer.astro:478 unfundedReal + "in today's prices" ← income unpaid insurance-analyzer.astro:573 lastPremiumReal + "in today's prices" ← last premium ₹59,190

Two bold figures, four hundredths apart, on one screen, sharing a label. The reader's first thought is that one is a typo of the other. The frame-carrying discipline from T8 was applied correctly — the failure is that it was applied to the unit without also disambiguating the quantity.

Every "in today's prices" figure needs its noun on the same line: "₹9.14 lakh more, in today's prices" vs "₹9.43 lakh paid out, in today's prices". And "THE SAME MONEY" as a heading is genuinely ambiguous — it reads as a claim of equivalence when it means "this sum, re-expressed".

F-13 Minor

"TRUE YIELD (XIRR)" is the analyst's vocabulary, not the reader's

The answer layer gets this exactly right — "The policy pays you back at 7.22% a year". Then the panel below reintroduces the jargon it just successfully avoided, as a 10px all-caps label. The numbered sections ("1. The Insurance Policy's Promise", "2. The Safe DIY Route", "3. The Growth DIY Route") also imply a sequence the reader is meant to walk, when they are really three parallel options.

"What the policy pays you back, a year (XIRR)" — the term in parentheses, for the reader who wants to verify, never as the label. And drop the numbering: these are three routes, not three steps. Numbering that does not encode a real sequence is decoration.

Part III — Cognitive load

Five moves that would carry the whole product

Your doctrine already contains the right principles. What is missing is their application to pixels rather than to prose. These five are ordered by leverage.

  1. Finish the rung pattern on the two unreformed pages. The tax calculator and insurance analyser have an excellent answer sitting on top of the product you were replacing. Every complaint in Part II about those two pages is really this one complaint. Nothing else in this report returns as much.
  2. One idea per card, and let the card be as tall as that takes. Today's cards are stacks of three or four micro-labelled figures. The reader has to work out which one is the point. Give each card a single sentence that says what it means and one figure that carries it; move the rest into the rung. The SIP engine's three lever cards already do this — they are the model.
  3. Make every number state its noun and its frame in the same breath. F-04 and F-12 are the same defect at different scales. Adopt one rule: quantity + amount + frame, always in that order, never split across a label and a value. "Left over at the end: ₹52.52 lakh in the rupees of that year" is unambiguous; "THE SAME MONEY / ₹9.43 Lakh / in today's prices" is not.
  4. Set a floor on type and a ceiling on emphasis. Nothing meaningful below 13px (F-01); one all-caps element per card (F-03); one accent colour per card. Right now the SWP expert rung has emerald, amber, rose and gold all signalling status within a single viewport, so none of them signals anything.
  5. Give the answer somewhere to go. Every calculator currently ends at the answer. A reader who learns their plan is tight has no next step — no way to save it, share it, come back to it, or reach the article that explains it. This is where the product currently leaks its entire audience, and it is the bridge to Part V.

Part IV — Content

Fifty-four articles, no citations, and twelve sharing one photograph

The writing is strong. The Loss That Never Happened opens with a concrete ₹10 lakh investor, uses a bonus issue to build the illusion, then resolves it with s.94(8) — real explanatory structure with bespoke components rather than stock diagrams. The Price You Didn't Receive runs 5,949 words with fifteen custom illustrations. This is genuinely better than most of what ranks for these queries today.

Four problems, in order of how much they cost you:

F-14 Blocker

Zero external citations across all 54 tax articles

$ grep -c "http" src/content/direct-tax/*.mdx | grep -v ":0" (no output — not one article links to anything)

Fifty-four articles making specific statutory claims — penalty rates, section numbers, thresholds, deadlines — and not one links to incometax.gov.in or indiacode.nic.in. For Google this is the clearest possible signal of unsourced YMYL content. For an AdSense reviewer assessing "low value content" on a finance site it is close to decisive. And for a reader it means your most defensible asset — that you are actually right — is invisible.

Your own launch gate already knows this instinct is correct: it records two tax rules as "INFERRED, not cited — do not state them as fact", because sol-042 happened when a confident note was backwards and nobody could check it. Extend that discipline to the reader.

Add a sources array to the content schema and render a "Where this comes from" block at the foot of every article. Three to five links each. This is the highest-value content work available and it needs no new writing.

F-15 Blocker

Twelve articles share one stock image — visibly, on your main index page

$ grep -h "^coverImage:" src/content/direct-tax/*.mdx | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn 12 coverImage: "hero-fallback.jpg" 1 coverImage: "tax-regime-decision.jpg" 1 coverImage: "sft-audit-relief.jpg" ... (41 others, one each)

In your Tax Code Vault screenshot, cards Q53, Q52, Q51, Q50 and Q49 all show the same glowing-book photograph. Five identical thumbnails in one grid is the visual signature of a content farm, on the page a reviewer is most likely to land on. It undercuts 54 genuinely researched articles in a single glance.

Twelve images. If generating them is expensive, a typographic cover — the section number set large on a navy field, per category — is cheaper, faster, more distinctive, and cannot look like stock. That would also be a stronger house style than photographs.

F-16 Major

No human author anywhere on the site

ArticleLayout.astro:28 sets "author": {"@type": "Organization", "name": "FinSight India"}. The content schema has no author field. No byline, no credentials, no photograph, no "who writes this" page.

Google's guidance for YMYL content asks who is responsible and what qualifies them. "FinSight India" answers neither. This is also the difference between a site AdSense approves and one it files under mass-produced content — which is now an explicit policy category, and one that AI-assisted publishing pipelines land in by default.

A real named author with a real biography, @type: Person in the schema, a byline on every article, and an author page. If the pipeline is AI-assisted, say so plainly and describe the review process — that reads as integrity, and it matches how the rest of this project already documents itself.

F-17 Minor

Dates are strings, so no article has a machine-readable date

content.config.ts:11 updatedDate: z.string() → "August 2026" ArticleLayout.astro:23 articleJsonLd — no datePublished, no dateModified

Tax content is intensely time-sensitive; "which financial year is this about?" is the reader's first question and Google's too. Rich results for Article require these fields.

z.coerce.date(), then emit datePublished and dateModified in the JSON-LD and a visible "Applies to FY 2025-26" badge.

One further note on depth. Thirty-eight of the 54 articles sit between 1,000 and 1,500 words; the five best run 2,800 to 6,200. Your launch gate already records that 13 articles fail the R2 screen and 26 fail R7. I would not publish the thin ones at all until they are brought up — 30 excellent articles is a far stronger AdSense application, and a far stronger site, than 54 uneven ones.

Part V — What to build

Eight ideas, ordered by what they'd actually do for you

1 · The homepage answers a question before you ask for anything

Replace the static "What you leave with" aside with one live input: "I have saved ₹___ and need ₹___ a month." Two fields, and the sentence completes itself as they type — "your money would typically last 23 years." Then: "See the full plan →".

You already have the engine, the copy register, and the worker. This converts the homepage from a description of the product into the product, and it is the single strongest thing you could do for both conversion and time-on-page.

2 · "What changed this year" — the annual-update engine

Every February, forty million Indians ask the same question after the Budget and get it answered by news articles that are obsolete in a week. A permanent page — "Budget 2026: what changed for you" — with the reader's own income filled in and last year's tax beside this year's, is a recurring traffic event you can own. It uses the tax engine you already have. Nobody does this well because everyone treats the Budget as news rather than as a calculator input.

3 · Save and return, without an account

Encode the inputs in the URL. "Copy my plan link" gives the reader something to keep, mail to their spouse, and come back to in six months. It costs no backend, breaks no privacy promise (the numbers never touch your server — they are in the reader's own clipboard), and it turns every serious user into a distribution channel. This is the fix for Part III's fifth move.

4 · The one-page plan, as a real document

You have already paid the 916 KB for html2pdf (F-05). Presently it exports the dashboard. Make it export the answer: one page, the sentence, the three levers, the assumptions, the date. That is a thing a person shows their spouse or their CA — and a CA seeing your assumptions block is the best marketing you could buy.

5 · Article to calculator, with the numbers carried across

The insurance article should end with the analyser pre-loaded with that article's example policy. The bonus-stripping piece should end in a workbench with its ₹10 lakh example filled in. You already have workbench components in src/components/mdx/ — they are just not wired to carry state. This is the loop that makes the articles worth having: research feeds the tool, the tool proves the research.

6 · "What if life happens?" — the shock rung

Already in your launch gate as Rahul's idea, unscoped. It deserves building. A lump-sum shock — a wedding, an operation, a parent's care — is the thing that actually breaks retirement plans, and no Indian calculator models it. It is liquidity risk rather than market risk, it is emotionally concrete in a way volatility never is, and it is a genuine differentiator.

7 · A comparison the industry will not publish

"What your bank's ₹1 lakh/year policy actually pays back" — the analyser run across the ten most-sold traditional plans, published as a standing table. Every one of those XIRRs will land between 4% and 6%. This is the highest-link-earning page you could publish, because it is a fact nobody selling those products can afford to state. Handle it carefully: use published benefit illustrations, state the assumptions, describe products rather than accusing companies.

8 · Show the failures, not just the score

"In 7,025 out of 10,000 scenarios your money lasted" is abstract. A small chart of 100 dots, ~30 of them dark, with "these are the futures where you run out — the earliest at year 14" makes probability into something a person can feel. You have the path data already; you are currently reducing it to a percentage and discarding the rest, which is dd-010 pointing straight at an unbuilt feature.

F-19 Major

No consent mechanism, which is mandatory for AdSense in India

Google requires a certified Consent Management Platform for EEA/UK traffic, and India's DPDP Act 2023 imposes its own consent duties. There is no banner, no CMP, no cookie controls anywhere in src/.

A Google-certified CMP before applying. Choose the most privacy-preserving defaults — it fits the "private by design" promise you already make on the homepage, and that promise is a real asset worth not undermining.

Part IX — The world

Can this win? Honestly: not as a calculator site. Possibly as something else.

What you are up against

The Indian personal-finance web has three tiers, and all three are hostile to a new entrant on the terms you are currently competing.

The distribution platforms — Groww, Zerodha, ET Money, INDmoney, Angel One — give calculators away free because the calculator is a customer-acquisition cost, not a product. They have domain authority you cannot approach, app installs in the tens of millions, and no need for the calculator itself to earn anything. The tax-filing platforms — ClearTax, Quicko, TaxBuddy — own every tax-calculator SERP for the same reason. The publishers — Moneycontrol, Economic Times, Value Research, Freefincal — own the informational queries with twenty years of accrued authority.

Against that, "a better SWP calculator" is not a business. There are hundreds of SWP calculators. The marginal reader cannot tell that yours is correct and theirs is not, because the wrong ones look confident and load faster.

What you actually have that they do not

Three things, and they are not the things the site currently leads with.

First: you can afford to be right. Every one of those competitors is funded by selling a product the calculator evaluates. That is why every retirement calculator in India shows a flat 12% return and a comfortable outcome — not through incompetence but because the honest answer ("about 1 in 3 futures run dry") does not sell a SIP. Your insurance analyser telling someone their bank's guaranteed plan pays 7.22% is a sentence no funded competitor can publish. That constraint is your moat, and it is the only one you have.

Second: the correctness discipline is real and it is unusual. A solutions ledger, cross-surface agreement tests, a doctrine with stable ids, and a habit of recording the defect that flatters you rather than quietly fixing it. Nobody in this market works this way. It is currently invisible to readers — /solutions and /standards exist but read as engineering logs. Turned outward, in plain language, "here is every mistake we found in our own calculator and what it changed" is a trust asset no competitor can copy without admitting their own.

Third: the writing register. "You would typically finish with ₹1.28 crore left — but this plan is tight" is a voice, and it is a better one than anything in the category.

My honest assessment of the odds

As an AdSense-funded calculator site: low, maybe 10–15%. The economics need roughly 500,000 monthly pageviews to produce a modest income, against competitors with structural advantages you cannot buy. The main risk is not that the product is bad — it is better than most — but that the effort is going into being more correct in a market that does not reward correctness at the margin, and that the packaging costs you the readers the correctness was for.

As a trusted independent voice with a narrow, sharp position: considerably better, perhaps 35–40%. "The site that tells you what your insurance policy actually pays" is a position nobody occupies, it is defensible precisely because it is unfundable by the incumbents, and it has natural distribution — people forward that answer to their family. The calculators then serve the position rather than being the product.

The strongest asset may not be the website at all. Your own memory notes record that the articles are the substrate and the autonomous production system is the actual project. That is probably right. A pipeline that reliably produces correct, well-designed, well-written financial explainers — with a doctrine, a solutions ledger and a gate — is a more valuable and more defensible thing than any one site it produces. If that is the real bet, then FinSight's job is to be the proof it works, and this report's Part I–IV are about making that proof presentable.

The one thing I would change about the strategy

You are building five calculators broadly. I would build one narrowly and completely: the insurance analyser. It has the sharpest question, the most surprising answer, the most emotional stakes, the least competition, the clearest sharing behaviour, and it is the one where being unfunded is an unfair advantage rather than a handicap. Take that one to genuinely finished — the answer, the article, the XIRR comparison table, the shareable link — and let the other four be good supporting evidence.

Depth over breadth is already your stated doctrine. It applies to the product surface as well as to the prose.

The difficulty of packaging is ours to absorb, never theirs. You have absorbed the difficulty of being correct. The packaging is the half that is left.
